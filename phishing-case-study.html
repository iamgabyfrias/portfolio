The problem

Knowledge that did not change behavior

A corporate client in finance and accounting was consistently failing phishing simulations, especially the ones that mimicked their own industry or looked like they came from HR. The usual security-awareness training was not changing what people actually did under pressure. Employees were downloading malware-laden apps, opening attachments from compromised contacts, and responding to fake HR messages. The gap was not information. It was behavior.

Design process

From baseline to durable habit

  1. Baseline

    Ran an initial phishing simulation in KnowBe4 to measure the real vulnerability level before any training, so improvement could be proven, not assumed.

  2. Scenario development

    Built phishing scenarios drawn from the attacks this workforce was actually likely to see, including industry-specific lures and HR spoofs.

  3. Interactive training

    Delivered live virtual sessions over Zoom built around participation and decision-making rather than passive slides.

  4. Monthly reinforcement

    Kept running simulations each month to track change and find where people still needed support.

Results

A measurable, sustained shift

Outcome

40.8% → 3.4% phish-prone rate over six months of monthly simulations and targeted follow-up.

More telling than the number, employees began reaching out on their own to verify suspicious messages. That is the sign the awareness had actually stuck, internalized judgment rather than one-time compliance.

I'm now more conscious about what's emailed to me, and I've emailed you to make sure messages weren't scams.Training participant

What made it work

Lessons for the next program

Relevance

Scenarios mirrored the real threats this workforce faced, so the training never felt abstract.

Interactive over passive

Live sessions with immediate feedback outperformed video modules by a wide margin.

Time and repetition

Durable change took six months of consistent reinforcement, not a single session.

Trust

A no-judgment space where people could ask "is this a scam?" was what got them engaging.
