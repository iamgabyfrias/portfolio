The problem

Making abstract ideas concrete for young minds

How do you teach machine learning to eight-year-olds? The concepts are abstract, and most tech education for this age either loses students or waters the ideas down to nothing. The curriculum had to stay accurate and age-appropriate at once, and it had to leave room for critical thinking about AI's limits and biases, not just its capabilities.

Teaching through analogy

Starting from what kids already know

Recognizing a cat

Children learn to spot a cat from its traits, whiskers, pointy ears, a meow. AI learns the same way, by finding patterns. And since some of those traits are shared with other animals, the analogy opens up why AI sometimes gets it wrong.

Predicting an election

Using Mexico's presidential election, students predicted the winner would be a man, based on past results. The winner was a woman. In one move, it showed that AI reflects the data it is given and is not always right.

The "Strike a Pose" project

Students train their own model

The core activity has students train an AI model to recognize emotions through body language.

  1. Choose an emotion and pose

    Groups pick a feeling, happy, sad, excited, frustrated, and the poses that express it.

  2. Build training data

    Teams create at least two poses per emotion and take multiple photos to train their model.

  3. Test the model

    Students check whether the AI recognizes the emotion in new poses, and see where it succeeds and where it fails.

  4. Improve and reflect

    Groups compare results, share what they noticed, and refine their models.

Results

Reach, and real understanding

Outcome

Reached 10,000+ students worldwide through Girls Who Code, with a framework teachers could run without deep technical knowledge.

Students came away able to talk about AI bias and accuracy, understanding that a model can be confidently wrong when its data is skewed. That is a rare thing to land with eight-year-olds, and it came from building, not lecturing.
